Concrete Cube Failure: Causes, Prevention and Responsibility

An evidence-led look at why 23 of 56 columns failed while all 106 footings passed, and how to stop it happening again.

02

Potential causes

Mix / RMC supply

  • Wrong grade or w/c ratio
  • Low cement
  • Aggregate quality
  • Admixture dosage
  • Long transit time and retempering

Site placing

  • Water added to ease pumping
  • Free fall
  • Segregation
  • Delay

Compaction and curing

  • Congested bars
  • Weak vibration
  • Honeycombing
  • Leaky forms
  • Poor curing
  • Early stripping
  • Hot weather

Sampling and testing

  • Cubes not made at discharge
  • Poor mould handling
  • Early movement
  • Sun exposure
  • Lab or machine error
03

Investigation process

  1. RecordsBatch tickets, slump, batching / discharge times, cube register.
  2. CompareThe 23 failed column results with passing columns and footing records — shared dates, trucks, plant batches and gangs.
  3. Site recordsCuring, stripping, cube storage and transport.
  4. GradeSpecified column grade vs supplied grade.
  5. ScreeningRebound hammer and UPV for mapping.
  6. CoresCore testing per IS 516 (as referenced in the deck).
  7. AcceptanceCheck against IS 456 acceptance criteria.
  8. DecisionStructural engineer decides to retain, strengthen or rebuild.
  9. Lab auditAudit laboratory and machine calibration.
04

Prevention

Approved mix design and trial mixes with margin over fck

Plant audit and calibration

Pre-pour checklist: formwork tightness, cover blocks, bar clearance, vibrator ready

Slump and time check per truck; no site water

Limited free fall; chute / tremie where suitable; layered compaction

Independent QA/QC sampling at discharge

Cube mould storage and curing procedure

Column curing at least 7–10 days (deck proposal)

7-day results as early warning — act before 28 days

Signed pour card per element; cube traceability

05

RMC owner's responsibilities

  • Define the boundary between plant supply / discharge and site placing, compaction and curing
  • Joint sampling at plant and site; duplicate cubes and records
  • Batch records, delivery tickets, time logs and photos
  • No water addition; record any breach
  • Plant quality controls, calibration, material testing; site quality representative for critical column pours
A liability boundary does not remove the need to establish the actual cause.

07

Reflection & conclusion

What I take from PS6 is the discipline of not deciding too early. The contrast — columns failing, footings passing, one supplier — points somewhere, but it does not prove anything on its own. The investigation has to let the records speak before anyone is held responsible.

Observations

  • Because the supplier is the same, the useful comparison is between the failed and passing pours: dates, trucks, batches and gangs.
  • Screening tools (rebound hammer, UPV) map the problem; cores and the structural engineer decide it.
  • Many listed causes sit at the handover between plant and site — which is why the RMC boundary has to be written down.
  • 7-day results are the earliest point to act; waiting for 28 days loses time.

Key takeaways

  • Evidence first, responsibility second.
  • Quality responsibility is shared across plant, site and laboratory.
  • Prevent recurrence with a pre-pour checklist, discharge sampling, curing control and traceable pour cards.

Team conclusion retained: records and tests establish the cause, the structural engineer decides on members, and prevention controls apply to all future pours. The RMC boundary defines responsibility without replacing the investigation.
